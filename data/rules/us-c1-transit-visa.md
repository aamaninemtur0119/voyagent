Source page: "Transit Visa," U.S. Department of State, travel.state.gov (Other Visa Categories section).

**What it is**: Transit (C) visas are nonimmigrant visas for persons traveling in immediate and continuous transit through the United States en route to another country, with few exceptions. "Immediate and continuous transit is defined as a reasonably expeditious departure of the traveler in the normal course of travel as the elements permit and assumes a prearranged itinerary without any unreasonable layover privileges." If the traveler seeks layover privileges for purposes other than transit through the United States — such as to visit friends or engage in sightseeing — the traveler must instead qualify for the visa type required for that purpose (i.e., a B visa).

**Alternatives to C-1**: If you already hold a valid visitor (B) visa, you may be able to use it to transit the United States instead of applying for a C-1. If you are a citizen of a Visa Waiver Program (VWP) participating country, you may be able to transit the United States under the VWP instead.

**Travel purposes that require a Transit (C) visa — examples given on the page**:
- A foreign citizen traveling to another country who will have a brief layover in the United States when the only reason for entering is to transit.
- A passenger embarking from a foreign port on a cruise ship or other vessel proceeding to another country (not the U.S.) that makes port in the U.S. with no intention of landing.
- A crewmember traveling to the U.S. as a passenger to join a ship or aircraft they will work on — this requires a crewmember D visa as well, most often issued as a combination **C-1/D visa**.
- A crewmember transiting the U.S. to join a vessel engaged in foreign trade for lightering activities (ship-to-ship transfer of liquid cargo) not exceeding 180 days — requires a combination **C-4/D-3** visa.
- A foreign citizen in immediate and continuous transit to/from the United Nations Headquarters District under the UN Headquarters agreement requires a **diplomatic transit (C-2)** visa; travel is limited to the immediate New York City vicinity.

**Travel purposes NOT permitted on a Transit (C) visa — examples given on the page**:
- A layover for a primary purpose other than transit (e.g., visiting friends, sightseeing) requires a visitor (B) visa instead.
- A "coasting officer" (a foreign ship's officer granted home leave while the vessel is in U.S. ports, provided the vessel does not remain in U.S. waters more than 29 days) generally requires a B visa, not a C-1.
- A crewmember on a private yacht cruising in U.S. waters for more than 29 days generally requires a B visa.
- An officer/employee of a designated international organization assigned to the U.S. travels on a G-4 visa, not a C-1.

**How C-1 differs from B-1/B-2**: The B-1/B-2 visitor visa is for business or tourism purposes where the traveler's destination is the United States itself. The C-1 (transit) visa is specifically for travelers whose intended destination is a *different* country, and the U.S. stop is only an immediate, continuous, prearranged layover. Anyone whose reason for being in the U.S. is anything other than pure transit (sightseeing, visiting people, extended business, etc.) needs a B visa, not a C-1, even during what would otherwise be a layover.

**Application process** (same DS-160-based process as other nonimmigrant visas):
1. Complete the online Nonimmigrant Visa Application, Form DS-160, and print the confirmation page.
2. Upload a photo meeting the Photograph Requirements as part of the DS-160.
3. Schedule a visa interview, generally at the U.S. Embassy/Consulate in the country where the applicant lives. Interviews are generally required, with certain limited exceptions; a consular officer may still require an interview of any applicant. Wait times vary by location, season, and visa category.
4. Pay the non-refundable visa application fee: **$185** (the "Application Fee" figure stated on the page). A visa issuance fee may also apply "if applicable to your nationality."
5. Gather required documents: passport valid for travel to the U.S. (valid at least 6 months beyond the intended period of stay, unless exempted by a country-specific agreement — each person needing a visa submits a separate application), DS-160 confirmation page, fee payment receipt (if required before interview), photo.
6. Additional documentation may be requested to establish: that the purpose of entry is transit while traveling onward to another country and that the applicant intends to depart the U.S.; ability to pay all costs while in the U.S.; and residence in the home country / intent to return there. Evidence of onward travel arrangements/permission to enter the next country, employment, or family ties may help establish this.
7. Attend the visa interview: a consular officer determines whether the applicant qualifies, and for which visa category. Ink-free digital fingerprint scans are usually taken during the interview.
8. After the interview, the application may require further administrative processing; the consular officer will inform the applicant if this is needed.

**Gap/caveat — maximum authorized stay length not stated on this page**: This official page does **not** state a specific maximum number of days a C-1 holder may remain in the United States in transit. The figure "29 days" does appear on this page, but only in the context of exceptions that require a *B visa instead of C-1* (the "coasting officer" and private-yacht-crew scenarios above) — it is not stated as a general C-1 authorized-stay duration. A widely-repeated secondary-source claim that "C-1 has a 29-day maximum stay" was NOT found stated as such on this official page during this session, and should not be presented to a user as a travel.state.gov-confirmed fact without further verification (e.g., of the actual admission period CBP grants at the port of entry, which is a separate DHS/CBP determination not addressed by this State Department page).

**Other notes from the page**:
- No guarantee of visa issuance — applicants should not make final travel plans or buy tickets before visa issuance.
- A valid U.S. visa in an expired passport remains valid and can be used together with a new valid passport.
- Some applicants renewing a visa in certain classes may qualify for the Interview Waiver Program (IWP), which allows renewal without an in-person interview, depending on the specific embassy/consulate.
- Misrepresentation or fraud in obtaining a visa may result in permanent visa refusal or denial of entry.
- If refused under INA section 214(b), an applicant may reapply if they believe circumstances have changed or they have additional evidence of qualification.
- **Citizens of Canada and Bermuda do NOT require visas to transit the United States.**

No specific document checklist beyond the above was itemized on this page for the C-1 category specifically (documents listed are the general nonimmigrant visa application requirements plus the transit-specific evidence noted above).
